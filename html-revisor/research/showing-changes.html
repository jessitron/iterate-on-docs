Showing changes to a rendered document

How others show removals and moves, and what fits html-revisor. Research for Jess, 2026-11-12.

The short version. The field has three places to put a removal: in place (the old content, struck or faded), a marker where it was (a small glyph you hover to see what went), and the margin (a note beside the line). Word, Google Docs, and Wikipedia's visual diff show removals in place. Ink & Switch's lab liked a marker best for prose and a ghost for drawings. Moves are a pair everywhere they are handled: a "from" spot and a "to" spot, linked by a name. The "from" spot is the deleted spot you asked for; Word's file format and Wikipedia both have one.

My recommendation: a tombstone as the lasting mark for a removal, drawn in the overlay, and a peek that puts the original back in place, grayed, only while you look at it. A move is a tombstone that points to where the element went. Details and drawings in Recommendations.

The vocabulary the field uses

Word Meaning Where you'll see it
Edit script / delta The list of operations that turns the old version into the new one. For trees the standard set is insert, delete, update, move. Tree diff papers (Chawathe et al.), XyDiff, GumTree, DeltaXML
Change awareness "The ability of individuals to track the asynchronous changes made to a collaborative document or surface by other participants over time." Tam & Greenberg 2004
Workspace awareness Knowing who is in the shared space, where, and doing what, in real time. Change awareness extends it to the past. Gutwin & Greenberg 2002
Highlighting vs annotation vs overview Three ways to show a change. Highlighting marks the content in place. Annotation is a note that points to it, often in a margin. Overview is a whole-document picture (a minimap, marks in the scroll bar). Tam & Greenberg's survey of tools
Inline vs side-by-side One view with both versions merged into it, or two views next to each other. diff2html, Figma branch review, every code review tool
Markup / redline The visible marks of track changes: underline for inserted, strikethrough for deleted. Word, legal review
Balloon / margin note / card A box in the margin that holds a deletion or a comment, with a line to its spot. Word, Google Docs
Change bar / changed line A vertical line in the margin next to any line that changed. Says "something here" and nothing more. Word's Simple Markup, printed standards documents
Move from / move to The two halves of a move: the spot it left and the spot it arrived. Linked by a shared name. Word (OOXML moveFrom/moveTo)
Ghost A deleted object drawn faded where it was. Ink & Switch's diagram diff
Tombstone A marker that stands where a deleted thing was. In CRDTs (the data structures behind live co-editing) it is the record that keeps a deleted item's position so other edits can refer to it. CRDT literature; I use it here for a visible marker
Persistent IDs (XIDs) IDs that stay on a node across versions, so matching old to new is a lookup, not a guess. XyDiff (Cobéna, Abiteboul, Marian 2002). html-revisor's data-hrv is this.
Change blindness People miss large changes to a scene when the change happens out of view or during a flicker. Removals are the hardest to notice: nothing is there to draw the eye. Rensink, O'Regan & Clark 1997

What each system chose, and why

Word track changes

  • Defaults: insertions underlined, deletions struck through, both in the author's color. A change bar sits in the margin ("Changed lines: Outside border").
  • Moves are tracked by default. "Moved from" is double strikethrough; "moved to" is double underline. In the file format, the two halves are linked by a shared w:name on moveFromRangeStart and moveToRangeStart. A "move to" with no matching "move from" "shall be treated as inserted, rather than moved." So a move is a deletion and an insertion that know about each other.
  • Tables get cell colors, not text marks: inserted cells light blue, deleted pink, merged yellow, split orange. Word did not try to make strikethrough work for structure.
  • Three levels of display. Simple Markup hides the marks and leaves only the red change bar in the margin; click the bar to see all. All Markup shows everything inline. Show revisions in balloons moves deletions out of the text into margin notes ("Deleted: Monday") and keeps insertions inline.
  • Why: Word's job is review before acceptance. The old text has to stay visible until someone accepts.

Google Docs suggestion mode

  • Deleted text stays in place, struck through in the suggester's color, until accepted. Each suggestion also gets a card in the right margin with a check (accept) and an X (reject).
  • Tools > Review suggested edits steps through them one at a time, or accepts or rejects all.
  • No move tracking. A move is a deletion here and an insertion there.
  • For "what changed since I last looked", Docs once had "See new changes" in the File menu, which highlighted changes since your last visit with a count to step through. New comments get a blue dot. Drive's newer "Catch me up" summarizes changes in words.

Wikipedia: the visual diff (VisualEditor)

This is the closest match to html-revisor: a diff of a rendered, structured document, in the browser, built for non-programmers.

  • Removed content stays in place, as itself: red background, line-through. The CSS marks the element with data-diff-action="remove"; it does not wrap it. So a removed table, image, or list item is still a table, image, or list item, in its own parent. Captions get their own rule because they draw outside the parent's box.
  • Inserted: green background. Changed (attributes, a resized image): blue, with the old version hidden and a sidebar note that describes the change in words.
  • Moves: the moved element gets a gray background and an arrow, ↑ or ↓, at its left edge. Since 2020 the sidebar labels it "Moved up" or "Moved down". The algorithm keeps "the maximum possible number of nodes unmoved" by a longest increasing subsequence: the same idea as recommendation 4 in CONCURRENCY.html.
  • Moves are detected only among siblings, "not within a node yet."
  • Known gaps, from its bug tracker: "the move indicator is really tiny" and "the place from where it was moved is not shown." That second one is your deleted-spot point. Wikipedia users asked for it and it is still open as a design task.
  • The text diff (wikidiff2) added a move indicator with a tooltip, "Paragraph was moved," and requests to draw an arrow linking the two spots.
  • Why visual: editors "can see changes without needing to understand wikitext," and moves read as moves, not as a delete plus an add. The page also admits visual diffs "have issues with complex changes to tables."

HTML diff libraries: W3C htmldiff, htmldiff.js, DaisyDiff, lxml, visual-dom-diff

  • All of them show removals in place. Most wrap removed text in <del> and added text in <ins>. DaisyDiff uses spans with classes (diff-html-removed, red with line-through) and Dojo tooltips that step back and forth between changes. visual-dom-diff wraps text in del/ins and puts a class (vdd-removed, vdd-added) on removed or added elements; an attribute change is vdd-modified, showing only the new attributes.
  • The trap they all meet: a wrapper element is not valid everywhere. You can't put a <del> around a <tr> or an <li>. Tools that diff by text tokens break on tables. Tools that diff the tree mark the element itself, as VisualEditor and visual-dom-diff do.
  • None handle moves. A W3C wiki comment notes that attribute-only changes may not show at all in a rendered view.

Tree and XML diff

  • The theory has had moves since Chawathe et al. (1996): an edit script of insert, delete, update, and move over an ordered tree. GumTree (code ASTs) and DeltaXML use the same four.
  • Finding a move without IDs is the hard part: you guess which old node is which new node. XyDiff gives each node a persistent ID (an XID), and then a move is a node whose ID now has a different parent or position. html-revisor has this already. Its moves are a lookup.
  • Presentation in XML tools is plain: color the tree view, often side by side. Nothing there beats VisualEditor for a rendered page.

Code review: GitHub, git, Beyond Compare, diff2html

  • Removed lines are red rows in the gutter view. Code review never hides a removal; code has no "final rendering" to protect.
  • git diff --color-moved colors moved lines in their own colors on both ends: oldMoved where they left, newMoved where they arrived. zebra alternates colors so two adjacent moved blocks can be told apart; dimmed-zebra dims the middle of a moved block, since only its edges are interesting. A block must be 20 alphanumeric characters to count.
  • GitHub, Bitbucket, and Beyond Compare do not detect moves. GitLab has an open proposal to use git's. Beyond Compare's answer is manual "Align With".
  • diff2html renders unified diffs as HTML, line by line or side by side. No moves.
  • Lesson for us: moves are shown at both ends, in a color that is neither add nor remove.

Design tools: Figma, Ink & Switch, Notion

  • Figma branch review lists added, edited, and removed layers by page, and shows each one side by side (before, after) or as an overlay with an opacity slider. A list beside the canvas, not marks on it. Users complain they can't pick out the exact property that changed.
  • Ink & Switch, Patchwork (2024 lab notebook): for prose, deletions as a backspace glyph; "Hover over the deletion glyph to see the removed text." This hover view was their favorite for a full document. A margin view showing replacements as "before → after" was also promising, since strikethrough gets noisy for replacements. For an overview, a minibar of where changes are. For diagrams: "a green glow for added shapes, and a translucent/ghost effect for deleted shapes." Their conclusion: "Diff visualization varies a lot more by document type."
  • Notion shows an agent's edits in a light blue track-change overlay you can show or hide. Its version history (2026) highlights edits and says whether a person or an agent made each one. I could not confirm how it draws a deletion.

Change awareness research

  • Gutwin & Greenberg (2002) broke workspace awareness into who, what, and where, for the present, plus a short list for the past: action history, artifact history, location history.
  • Tam & Greenberg (2004, 2006) extended the past part into a change awareness framework. Six questions: where, who, what, how, when, why. Three views: by artifact, by person, by workspace.
    • The first question is "Is anything different since I last looked at the work?" and it must be answered "in a very lightweight fashion." Then the person probes for detail. html-revisor's docket count answers the first question; the marks answer the probe.
    • For an artifact, their "where" questions are: "Where was this artifact (when I left)?", "Where is the artifact now?", "Where has this artifact been during the time that I have been away?" A removal answers the first and not the second. A move answers both. A tombstone is how you answer "where was it" once it's gone.
    • They sort display methods into diffs, annotations (margin notes), highlighting (in place), and overviews (scroll bar marks, miniatures). They note that showing everything overwhelms; the design job is choosing what matters.
    • "Why" is one of the six: html-revisor's --because is the why.
    • Readership history: who has seen what. They note that someone seeing a change without objecting is an implicit acceptance. html-revisor's ack is an explicit readership record.
  • Change blindness (Rensink et al. 1997): people miss changes that happen during a flicker or out of view. A swap into a page you are not looking at is that. A removal is the worst case: the eye has nothing new to land on. This is the case for marking removals at all, and for marking them where they were.
  • Edit wear and read wear (Hill et al. 1992): marks in the scroll bar where a document has been edited. An overview that fits beside html-revisor's docket, for later.

Accessibility of change markup

  • <del> and <ins> carry the ARIA roles deletion and insertion, with cite and datetime attributes. But most screen readers don't announce them. Adrian Roselli: "with the recent exception of <del> in NVDA with Chrome," these elements "are not announced by screen readers."
  • The common fix is text: del::before { content: " [deletion start] "; }, hidden from sight with a clip pattern so it stays in the accessibility tree. JAWS in Chrome has been reported to skip pseudo-element text, so real text in the DOM is safer.
  • ARIA 1.3 (draft) adds role="suggestion", which groups an insertion and a deletion into one change, with aria-details pointing at who made it and why. That maps onto a change plus its --because.
  • Color alone fails (WCAG 1.4.1). Atlassian has an open bug on Confluence page history for this. html-revisor's rule, a glyph or a word behind every color, already meets it.
  • For html-revisor: marks live in an overlay, invisible to a screen reader. A ghost in place would be real DOM; give it aria-hidden or an explicit "removed:" label, so a reader doesn't read old text as current.

How "mark as seen" works elsewhere

System Acknowledge Scope
Word Accept or reject each change; accept all. An accepted deletion vanishes. Per change, all
Google Docs Check or X on each card; Review suggested edits steps through them; accept all. Comments: blue dot until seen. Per change, all
GitHub PR "Viewed" checkbox per file. When the file changes again, it unchecks and says it changed since you viewed it. Per file
Notion AI Show or hide the change overlay. All
VisualEditor, htmldiff, git None. A diff is between two versions you pick; there is no "seen". n/a
Tam & Greenberg Readership history: a record of who saw what, which can stand for implicit acceptance. Per artifact

Two points carry over. First, a deletion mark is the one mark whose ack removes something from the page, not only a decoration. Word and Docs treat accepting a deletion as making it final. Second, GitHub's "changed since you viewed" is the same rule as html-revisor's: an ack holds until the content changes again.

What generalizes across element kinds

Your worry was right. The gap line in CONCURRENCY.html is drawn for a list of blocks stacked down the page. Here is each placement against the element kinds html-revisor pages hold.

Removed element Ghost in place Tombstone line in the gap Margin note
Paragraph in a section Works Works: horizontal line Works
List item in an ol Works; the ghost takes a number unless CSS stops it (counter-increment: list-item 0) Works Works
Table row Works: a tr is valid in its tbody; fade it and strike it Works: line across the table Points at the line, not the row
Table column (a cell in each row) Works: ghost cells A vertical line per row, or one line down the table No line to point at
Card in a grid or flex row Works; the others reflow while it shows Line runs across or down, depending on where the neighbors sit Weak
Shape in an svg Works: the Ink & Switch ghost. Opacity and a dashed stroke are valid on any svg element. No "gap" in a drawing. Needs the shape's old box instead: a dashed outline where it was. No
A whole section, many screens long A long gray block shoves the page down Works: one line, tip says "removed: § Steps (12 paragraphs)" Works
Last child, or all children Works Line at the end of the parent, or inside an empty one Works
Inline (a word, a link, a span) Works as <del> text, if you want word-level diffs A caret mark in the text Points at the line

Three patterns come out of this.

  1. Mark the element; don't wrap it. The ghost generalizes when it is the original element, same tag, in its own parent, with a class on it. That is what VisualEditor and visual-dom-diff do. Wrappers like <del> break in tables, lists, and svg.
  2. A line in the gap generalizes in position but not in shape. "After sibling X in parent P" is a fine address for any element. Drawing it needs the layout: horizontal between stacked blocks, vertical between side-by-side ones, and the old box for svg or absolute positions.
  3. The old box is the fallback that fits every kind. Where the element was on screen, captured before it left, works for a paragraph, a row, a card, and an svg shape. The client has it: setChildren in client.js removes the nodes the swap drops, and it can measure them first.

Recommendations for html-revisor

What html-revisor has that most of these tools lack: persistent IDs on every unit, a server that saw each change go through it, and a client that holds the old DOM at the moment of the swap. Moves are a lookup, not a guess. The old HTML and the old box are both in hand at swap time.

Option A: Ghost in place

The removed element stays in the page, grayed, struck or dashed, inert, wearing the green crop marks and a check tile. Ack takes the ghost out. A move leaves a ghost at the old spot with "moved ↓" and marks the element at its new spot "moved here ↑".

Build cost: high. The client today draws every mark in its overlay layer and never adds content to the page. A ghost is page content that isn't in the file. It needs: the removed HTML (the client has it at swap time; after a reload the server must send it, so the "seen" snapshot must keep HTML, not only fingerprints); a ghost marker that every DOM walk skips (client.js's isTool keeps nodes with an hrv- id through a swap, but setChildren would slide a kept ghost to the end of its parent, so it needs an anchor); its data-hrv stripped so IDs don't collide; and the server's domain modules must never see it, since a save or a markdown edit of the parent would carry it in. Every future feature that walks the page pays this tax.

Option B: Tombstone in the overlay, with a peek

A tombstone is a thin green mark at the spot where the element was, drawn in the overlay like the crop marks today, with its own check tile. Its tip says what went: "removed: Beside the trail, to build…" from the first words, or "removed: § Steps (12 paragraphs)". Peek (click the tombstone, or flip to it from the docket) puts the original back in place, grayed, for as long as you look; the next flip or Escape takes it out. A move is a tombstone that says "moved ↓" and points to the element at its new spot, which wears a "moved here" mark. Clicking either end scrolls to the other.

Build cost: medium. It fits how the client works: marks in the overlay, sized from boxes in the page. Pieces:

  • Domain. Two notice kinds, removed and moved, from the snapshot you saw and the page now. Removed: an ID in seen that is gone from the page, outermost only (a removed section covers what was in it). Its address: parent ID plus the ID of the nearest sibling before it that is still there. Moved: an ID whose parent changed, or which falls outside the longest increasing subsequence of its siblings' old order (what VisualEditor does). Its address: where it was, as for a removal. The parent stops wearing a mark for these, as in recommendation 2 of CONCURRENCY.html.
  • What went. For the tip, the first words of the unit you saw. For the peek, its HTML. The snapshot holds fingerprints today; keeping each unit's HTML (or the HTML of removed ones only, captured when the change lands) gives both. The server already plans a "view previous", which needs the same thing.
  • Drawing. Find the gap from the neighbors' boxes: if the sibling after sits below, a horizontal line; beside, a vertical line; none, the end of the parent. For an svg descendant, or when the client measured the element just before it left, a dashed outline of the old box. The old box only survives until layout changes, so it is the fallback, not the rule.
  • Peek. Insert a clone of the old HTML at the address, with an hrv- marker, inert, aria-hidden, and no data-hrv. Remove it on the next flip, Escape, or any swap. Because it never outlives one look, it never meets a save.

Option C: Margin notes

A change bar in the page's left margin at the height of the gap, like Word's Simple Markup, with a note on hover. Cheapest: a tile in the overlay at a y position. It can't point inside a table row or an svg, and a page whose content runs edge to edge has no margin. Good as a second channel (an overview strip by the docket, à la edit wear and Ink & Switch's minibar), not as the mark.

Recommendation: B.
  1. Tombstone for a removal: placed by parent and previous sibling, drawn as a line in the gap, or as a dashed old box where there's no gap. Tip: the first words of what went. One check tile. Ack deletes the tombstone and its stored HTML.
  2. Peek: the original in place, grayed, inert, for one look. This is "view previous" for a removal, so build it once for both.
  3. Move: a tombstone at the old spot ("moved ↓") paired with a mark at the new one ("moved here ↑, was after A"). One notice, two ends, one ack. Flipping lands on the new spot and draws a dashed link to the tombstone.
  4. A move whose old spot can't be drawn (its old parent is gone too) shows as new at its new spot. Word's file format makes the same rule: a move-to with no move-from "shall be treated as inserted."
  5. Later, an overview strip by the docket (option C's good half), when pages get long.

Open questions for Jess

  1. Is the peek enough of "the original grayed out", or do you want ghosts to stand until ack (option A) despite the cost?
  2. Should a tombstone survive a reload and a server restart? Today a restart forgets what you saw. A tombstone is the one mark whose loss loses information: after a restart, you'd never learn that something went.
  3. Where should the removed HTML live: in the seen snapshot for every unit (simple, more memory), or captured only when a change removes something (smaller, but a removal by editing the file on disk has no change to capture it)?
  4. A removed section: one tombstone for the section, or one per scope that was in it? I'd say one, with a count in the tip.
  5. Should flip order put a move's two ends next to each other, or treat the move as one stop that shows both?
  6. Word-level change inside a paragraph (struck words, <del> style) is out of scope here; the paragraph wears the mark. Is that still the line you want, or does the peek also want to show a paragraph's old words?
  7. For svg: is "a dashed outline of the old box" right for a drawing, or should a removed shape peek as itself, faded, the way Ink & Switch drew it?
